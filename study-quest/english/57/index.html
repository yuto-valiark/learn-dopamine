浦島太郎から百年分のメール

A Hundred Years of Mail

お手本音声

Years after they had first met Urashima, Nozomi received an envelope from the undersea palace. It contained a password, an address, and a stamp showing a smiling octopus. At the top of the password sheet was a warning: some messages had arrived a hundred years late. Kanato said that this sounded exactly like the palace's usual service.

They opened Urashima's old mailbox with his permission. Hundreds of messages appeared. Many started with, “Sorry for the late reply.” Most were about an old promise to meet a childhood friend. Urashima had never given up looking, but the town had changed. He had not been able to identify the meeting place from any of the pictures he had found.

Yuto showed them a folder of town photographs from the library archive. In the folder were pictures of the town that a shopkeeper's nephew had carried with him when he moved abroad. The original message from Urashima's friend gave just one clue. There was no return address or even the name of the district. At age ten, the friends had assumed they'd always know where the shop was. It said, “The white building has a window like a fish.” One picture showed a round window. “Probably not that one,” said Nozomi. She turned over the next photograph. “Wait. See that circle at the end of the long window? It's the fish's eye.”

Yuto recognized the street and marked the location on a map. The white building was now a bookshop. “I didn't know where to look for so long,” he said. “Now I've finally found the window.”

At the bookshop, Urashima asked the owner whether she knew who had built the window. She told him that her great-grandfather had put it in himself. He was Urashima's friend. She showed them a note that her family had kept through three generations. It said that an old friend might return one day and that he should be offered tea.

“Is that appointment still available?” asked Urashima.

“It's been available for a hundred years,” she said, getting up to put a kettle on.

They had tea by the window, just as Urashima and his friend had planned before the war. “He always said we'd meet again in a time of peace,” said Urashima. By the window was a small family memorial with his friend's name on it. The owner had never known him, but she knew the story about the visitor from the sea. She set the old note next to a photograph of her great-grandfather. “He kept it for one purpose,” she said. “So we'd know to offer you tea.” Before they left, Urashima wrote a reply to the original message and dated it that afternoon. Kanato volunteered to deliver it to the archive. He added a note in the margin: “Delivered on time by ordinary land post.”
